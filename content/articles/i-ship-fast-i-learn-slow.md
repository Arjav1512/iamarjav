# I Ship Fast. I Learn Slow.

In July, the AI coach in ChessMate stopped working. The coach was the whole point of the product, the part that explains in plain language why your moves were bad, and on the live site it was returning errors because an API key wasn't configured on the server. My website still said the product was live.

I didn't hear about it from a user. I found it in an audit, because almost nobody outside my own circle was using ChessMate. When the main feature of a product breaks and nobody complains, that tells you something. It just isn't anything good about the feature.

## Half a loop

My brother spends a lot of time on X, and he sends me what he finds there: people building in public, shipping rough versions, getting feedback, shipping again. His view is that in the age of AI, speed is one of the most important things you can have. Build it fast, put it out, learn from what happens, and do it again. The more times you go around, the faster your judgement grows.

On paper, I'm a good argument for him. I've built more than fifteen projects in about two years. As I wrote recently, I don't write 99% of my code; agents do. The time between having an idea and having a working version on a URL has gone from months to days.

But a loop has more than one step. Eric Ries called it build, measure, learn, and his point was never to make any one step fast. It was to cut the total time it takes to get all the way around. AI made my build step close to instant. It did nothing for the step where someone who actually has the problem uses the thing and tells me, in words or in what they do, whether it helped.

A loop is only as fast as its slowest step. In my projects, that step isn't slow. Mostly it doesn't happen.

When I finish something, I send it to friends and people close to me. They're kind, they try it, and they tell me what they think. I've posted most of my projects on X and LinkedIn as well. A few people found them that way, and nearly all of them already work in software. Their feedback is real, but it comes from people who already know how this kind of product is supposed to work, and those are exactly the people my products aren't for. ChessMate is for club players trying to get better. Very few of them have ever used it.

So I've run half the loop many times. I've almost never run all of it.

Without really noticing, I'd been timing myself from idea to deploy, because that's the number AI made look good. The number that matters starts when I ship and stops when someone the product was built for tells me something useful. By that clock, I'm not fast at all.

## Fifteen projects, one shape

When I put my projects side by side, a lot of them look alike. ChessMate analyses your games and tracks whether you're improving. Mirror was a journal that flagged distorted thinking and tracked your emotional patterns. PrepPilot tracks your studying. Digital Diary, my CS50 final project, tracked your browsing and gave you a productivity score. Each one watches what you do, tells you something true about it, and keeps score.

I used to think that was a theme. Now I think it's a symptom.

Naval Ravikant has a line I keep coming back to: it's "not 10,000 hours, it's 10,000 iterations." He's careful to say that iterating isn't the same as repeating. You do something, stop, look at how it went, and change it. For a product, the "look at how it went" part needs someone other than you.

Without real users, the only thing my next project could learn from was me. The two I talk about most, ChessMate and Mirror, were built mostly for my brother and me. So every new version was shaped by the same person's taste, and it kept coming out the same shape. I wasn't iterating. I was repeating, with better tools each time.

And I don't use Mirror or ChessMate anymore. By the time they were finished, even the user I understood best had moved on.

My brother learned the other way. He's a product designer, and early in his career he entered design contests on a crowdsourcing site. He entered 36 before he won one. After 64, he had won four.

By my standards, that's painfully slow. But every one of those designs was judged by the person it was made for: a real client choosing a real winner. Each round, he got an answer from the only person whose opinion counted. Mine are faster per attempt and judged mostly by friends. I used to assume I was the one learning faster. I wasn't, and it took me fifteen projects to see it.

## What I already know how to do

The strange part is that the missing step isn't a skill I lack. I spent five months on a sales internship at ElevenX, and most of it was cold calls. The job was to call people who hadn't asked to hear from me and work out what they actually needed. I've almost never done that for anything I've made.

I think part of the reason is that being turned down on behalf of someone else's company is easy. It's the job, and it isn't about you. Handing a stranger something you made and watching them not care is a different feeling. Sending things to friends and posting them on X felt like putting my work into the world. Looking back, it was mostly a way of putting it where nobody could say no to my face.

So this is what I'm changing. Before I start the next project, I'm going to find five people who have the problem and don't work in tech, and talk to them first. Calls, not a form. If what they tell me kills the idea, that's the loop working. I'll write about how it goes, including if it goes badly.

My brother was right that speed matters. I was just timing the wrong thing. The clock I care about now starts when I ship, and it stops when someone who needs the thing tells me the truth about it. For most of what I've built, it's still running.
